Classic grand piano

A recorded Yamaha C5 grand

A Yamaha C5, recorded. One dedicated sample per key per velocity layer — 88 × 16, repitched off the Salamander library with the instrument's own body response held still — played by an engine with per-key velocity response, stereo spread, a geometric room, and sympathetic resonance that accumulates while the pedal is down.

Play with a MIDI keyboard, or z…m and q…u on your keyboard; space is the pedal.

The library streams as you play: the first notes are ready in a few seconds, the rest arrives behind you. A MIDI keyboard can be connected before or after starting.

Keyboard — click to play (hit high on the key for more velocity), shift-click to lift its dampers silently

Hear the sympathetic resonance: shift-click C4, E4 and G4 to raise their dampers without striking them — they turn blue — then play C2 hard. The chord answers on its own, and it answers louder the harder you hit. Now hold the sustain pedal and play the same note repeatedly: the resonance piles up, because each strike adds to an energy accumulator that only leaks away at each string's own measured decay rate. Lift the pedal and every damper falls at once.

Velocity response — C4

Velocity → layer

Map which of the 16 recorded layers each velocity plays: click to add a point, drag to move, double-click to remove. This replaces the single layer-bias knob — because the whole range is drawable, every layer stays reachable. The blue dot is the last note you struck, on the layer it landed on. Where the line is flat, a wide band of velocities all play the same layer (and stay near its native level); where it is steep, the layer changes quickly. Per-key Layer bias still nudges this for one key.

Volume

By default (off) each layer plays at its own recorded level, with the level ramped smoothly between neighbouring layers so crossing a boundary changes the timbre but not the loudness — no abrupt jumps. Turn this on to override that with a hand-drawn velocity→level curve: click to add a point, drag to move, double-click to remove, pull the far-left point to silent for a true pianissimo. Per-key Level trim always applies.
Level comes from the curve; timbre comes from which of the sixteen recordings the velocity reaches for. They are separate on purpose — changing the curve never changes the tone, and changing the layer bias never changes the level. Both are per key: draw them on the right.

Sample map — every key × velocity layer, lit as it plays

The grid is the whole library: 88 keys across, the 16 velocity layers up (softest at the bottom, loudest at the top). A cell is brighter when that exact recording is resident in memory and dim when it has not been streamed in yet. When you play, the cell for the note and the layer its velocity reached for lights gold — so you can see which of the sixteen recordings is actually sounding — and fades once the key comes up.

Output & stereo

The Salamander recordings are reversed — a key on the right of the keyboard sits on the left of the stereo image — so left and right are swapped first, for every key and its release, damper and resonance sounds alike. Stereo invert flips that swap back, before spread and width, for when your speakers or the recordings disagree. After that, spread at 0 leaves each key at its own corrected position; move it right to push the treble further right and the bass further left, or left to mirror the whole keyboard. Width rotates each key's own stereo image rather than panning it, so a spread key does not collapse to one side.

Output EQ — after everything, including the room

Shelves at the ends, two bells in the middle: body around 300 Hz where a piano gets boxy, presence around 3 kHz where the hammer lives. The curve is drawn from the filters' own response, so what is on screen is what is running. It sits before the limiter, so a boost cannot sneak past it, and after the room, so it is the last word on tone — use the per-key Level trim for a single note that is wrong, not this.

Reverb

The one dry path, shared by both reverbs. At zero you hear only the reverbs.

1 · Early reflections — the modelled piano's room

Early reflections send expander — peaks in, quiet parts less
This reverb's own expander, on what it hears, not on what you hear. Below the threshold the send falls away ratio times faster than the note does — at 2:1, a passage 10 dB under the threshold reaches the reverb 20 dB down — so attacks and loud chords fill it and soft playing and the long tails of notes stay drier. Ratio 1 is off. Attack is how fast the send opens when the level rises — short lets a note's front in, longer keeps the hammer out and lets only the bloom in. Release is how fast it closes as the level falls — short cuts the send off with each note's decay, long lets it follow a phrase rather than a note. The meter shows the send level (the bar) and how far it is being pulled down right now (the red part).
The modelled piano's room, run once on an impulse and convolved: image-source early reflections — real paths off real surfaces, the source mirrored in each wall, stereo from the two ears' distances to every image — which is what places the piano in a room. Its own feedback-delay tail is held at 0.3 s and only knits the reflections together; the long tail is the hall's.

2 · Hall — the long tail

Hall send expander — peaks in, quiet parts less
The hall's own expander — the same controls as the early reflections' one, set independently, so one can take only the attacks while the other takes everything.
A late tail built the way a real one behaves, not a captured IR. Reverb time is the RT60 of the mids; every frequency then decays at its own rate — bass and treble are the RT at 125 Hz and 8 kHz as multiples of it, and the air takes more above that — which is what stops a synthetic tail sounding like hiss fading out. The tail starts sparse, separate arrivals thickening into a continuum over the build-up, and rises over the same time, rather than switching on as a wash. Impulse loads a captured hall instead (any audio file the browser can decode; kept in this browser, levelled to match); the shaping controls then apply only to the synthetic hall. Click it again to go back.
Both reverbs hang off the one send, and each has its own level, expander and switch. A reverb that is off stops convolving.

Resonance

Gold bars are the strings answering right now, as loud as their voices currently are. The lit background is every string whose damper is off, which is what the pedal actually does. Blue is what you are holding down.

Resonance level per key

How strongly each string answers, in dB, for all three kinds below: click to add a point, drag to move, double-click to remove, pull a point to the bottom to stop those strings answering at all. The top strings have no dampers (right of the green line), so they answer everything, pedal or not.

Sympathetic — free strings, by shared partials

Soundboard — strings around the key, pedal or not

Self — the struck key's own strings

All resonance voices

Every resonance voice plays the library's own recording of the string that answers, as recorded, and lasts as long as the note driving it: while the key is held, or while the pedal holds the note after the key is up. When that note stops, the voice fades over its kind's release.

Sympathetic strings are the ones whose dampers are off — pedal down, keys held, and the top strings — answering at the partials they share with the note: a fifth strongly, a tritone barely. A string answers at the partials that match, not at its note: C2 into a free G2 meets at G2's 2nd partial, so G2 rings an octave up and its own pitch never sounds, and a high note reaches a bass string only through its upper partials. Ring partials only plays just those partials (a steep high-pass under the lowest one driven); off plays the whole recording. Selectivity widens how far out of tune a partial may be and still answer. Near/far tilts it by distance (positive favours near strings) without changing the total. Pedal-up release is how fast they die when the pedal lifts and the dampers land.

Soundboard is the strings around the struck key picking it up through the bridge and the board, pedal or not: the nearest loudest, down by falloff. With the pedal up, this is what goes on sounding for its release after you let go of the key. Strings picks which of them answer, counted out from the key: every one, every 2nd, every 3rd... (12 is the octaves only).

Self is the struck key's own unison and duplex answering it: the same key's recording under the note.

Falloff and near/far are in dB per doubling of distance, the way vibration spreading from a point dies away: the nearest few strings stay close in level, an octave away is well down, and further out the curve flattens. The readout shows what that comes to at 1 semitone, an octave and two octaves.

Enter at starts each recording past the hammer knock, and fade-in brings it in gently, because what answers a strike is a string ringing, not another strike. Max voices keeps the loudest. A string struck again while it is still ringing is not restarted: it is turned up smoothly, the way a vibrating string is pushed harder. Cut crossfade is how long a voice takes to hand over when it does have to go — faded out to make room under max voices, or crossfaded into a fresh start when its recording has decayed too far to turn up.

A quick pedal change — up and straight back down — does not stop what the dampers had not finished stopping: it carries on from the level it had fallen to. Only resonance still louder than resume above comes back (in dB of that string struck mezzo-forte); the rest finishes fading. Lifting a damper never starts new resonance — only a strike does. Solo mutes everything else, to hear only what this section adds; it is never saved.

Release level against how long the key was held

The damper-release sound is the sound of a damper stopping something, so it depends on how much is left to stop — and a string four seconds into its decay has very little. The dashed blue line is C4's own decay, measured at build time: trace it and the law is physically right for that key. Salamander's SFZ does this as a flat dB per second; a real decay is not a straight line, and the first second matters far more than the eighth. The key-up thud does not follow by default — a key comes up the same way however long it was down — but the last slider is there if you disagree.

Mechanics

The sustain pedal is continuous, not a switch: half-pedalling shortens a note without stopping it, and CC 64 is read across its whole range. The damper-fall sound is picked by velocity and scaled by how long the key was held — a string ringing for four seconds has less left in it to stop. Cut notes below fades out a note once it has decayed under that level (dB of full scale, before the master), instead of letting it play its whole recording — twenty seconds in the bass — under the pedal or a held key, holding a voice the whole time. The level is estimated from each recording's decay measured at build time. At the far left it is off. Pedal-up sweep is how much later the lowest damper lands than the highest when the pedal comes up: the damper frame reaches the treble strings first and the bass last, spread evenly between them, and the notes, their damper sounds and the sympathetic resonance all follow it. Of the strings a pedal lift stops, the loudest few make a damper sound (dampers heard), at damper level of what a key release plays; a whole frame of them at full level is a wall of noise, not a piano. The limiter is a seatbelt, not a sound: it is doing nothing until eighty-eight undamped strings would have run out of headroom. Align sample starts lines up the attack of all 1408 recordings: each one was measured (sampled/tools/align.mjs) for how far into the file its attack front actually is, and it runs from 5 ms to 59 ms across this library — the build trims each head at a level threshold, which lines up where silence ends, not where the note arrives. With this on, a late recording is started further in and an early one is held back by the difference, so every key's attack lands the same distance after the key goes down and the keyboard feels even. Nothing is re-cut: it is an offset at playback, and Sample start in the Parameters panel nudges one key or one range on top of it. Release start trim shaves a fixed few ms off the front of every release and damper sample; round-robin adds a random few ms on top of that per play, so the same key let go twice does not replay the identical file and machine-gun. Release delay holds the note a millisecond past the key-up so its damper falls in step with the release sample, not a hair ahead of it. There is no pedal-action sample: Salamander's two pedal recordings are a mechanism being worked, with a room and a frame ringing in them that are not this room or this frame, and what they stand in for — the whole undamped frame lighting up as the pedal goes down — is something this engine does properly, string by string, under Resonance. They are not loaded.

Parameters — pick the keys once, then edit; the chart is a preview

Every slider moves whatever the strip at the top has selected — the whole instrument, a range you dragged, or one key — so there is one place where the question "which keys?" is answered. The three tiers add: a range edit is a departure from the global value, a key edit a departure from that, and zero always means "as shipped". A range is feathered at its edges, so raising the top octave and a half does not make one key suddenly different; set the edge fade to zero when you do want a hard edge, the way a real piano has one at the bass break. The charts on the right are previews, not editors — the gold line is the total across all three tiers, which is where a cliff shows up, and the caption names the biggest jump between neighbouring keys. Clicking one selects that key to edit. smooth rounds off the per-key layer only, so a deliberate single-key fix survives it unless you ask. Edits are stored in this browser and reload with the page.

Everything not in this list is one value for the instrument, because it is a property of the instrument rather than of a key: master, stereo spread and perspective, room, resonance, the envelope shapes, the EQ, and the mechanical noise levels. A Bézier shape cannot be a per-key offset — there is no meaningful "twenty percent of a curve" to add — but its duration can be, and the damper fall's already is: the shape is global, the time it takes is the Damper fall parameter here.
