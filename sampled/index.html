Piano Model X — Sampled

A Yamaha C5, recorded. One dedicated sample per key per velocity layer — 88 × 16, repitched off the Salamander library with the instrument's own body response held still — played by an engine with per-key velocity response, stereo spread, a geometric room, and sympathetic resonance that accumulates while the pedal is down.

The library streams as you play: the first notes are ready in a few seconds, the rest arrives behind you. Connect a MIDI keyboard before or after starting, or play with z…m / q…u. space is the sustain pedal.

Keyboard — click to play (hit high on the key for more velocity), shift-click to lift its dampers silently

Hear the sympathetic resonance: shift-click C4, E4 and G4 to raise their dampers without striking them — they turn blue — then play C2 hard. The chord answers on its own, and it answers louder the harder you hit. Now hold the sustain pedal and play the same note repeatedly: the resonance piles up, because each strike adds to an energy accumulator that only leaks away at each string's own measured decay rate. Lift the pedal and every damper falls at once.

Velocity → layer

Map which of the 16 recorded layers each velocity plays: click to add a point, drag to move, double-click to remove. This replaces the single layer-bias knob — because the whole range is drawable, every layer stays reachable. The blue dot is the last note you struck, on the layer it landed on. Where the line is flat, a wide band of velocities all play the same layer (and stay near its native level); where it is steep, the layer changes quickly. Per-key Layer bias still nudges this for one key.

Volume

By default (off) each layer plays at its own recorded level, with the level ramped smoothly between neighbouring layers so crossing a boundary changes the timbre but not the loudness — no abrupt jumps. Turn this on to override that with a hand-drawn velocity→level curve: click to add a point, drag to move, double-click to remove, pull the far-left point to silent for a true pianissimo. Per-key Level trim always applies.
Level comes from the curve; timbre comes from which of the sixteen recordings the velocity reaches for. They are separate on purpose — changing the curve never changes the tone, and changing the layer bias never changes the level. Both are per key: draw them on the right.

Sample map — every key × velocity layer, lit as it plays

The grid is the whole library: 88 keys across, the 16 velocity layers up (softest at the bottom, loudest at the top). A cell is brighter when that exact recording is resident in memory and dim when it has not been streamed in yet. When you play, the cell for the note and the layer its velocity reached for lights gold — so you can see which of the sixteen recordings is actually sounding — and fades once the key comes up.

Output & stereo

The Salamander recordings are reversed — a key on the right of the keyboard sits on the left of the stereo image — so left and right are swapped first, for every key and its release, damper and sympathetic-resonance sounds alike. After that, spread at 0 leaves each key at its own corrected position; move it right to push the treble further right and the bass further left, or left to mirror the whole keyboard. Width rotates each key's own stereo image rather than panning it, so a spread key does not collapse to one side.

Output EQ — after everything, including the room

Shelves at the ends, two bells in the middle: body around 300 Hz where a piano gets boxy, presence around 3 kHz where the hammer lives. The curve is drawn from the filters' own response, so what is on screen is what is running. It sits before the limiter, so a boost cannot sneak past it, and after the room, so it is the last word on tone — use the per-key Level trim for a single note that is wrong, not this.

Room

Not a captured impulse response — an image-source model of a shoebox for the early reflections (real path lengths off real surfaces, stereo from the geometry) and a dense, frequency-damped noise field for the diffuse tail, rendered to an impulse and convolved. The tail is noise rather than a feedback delay network on purpose: a handful of short delay lines rings like a spring, and noise has no period to ring at. The controls are geometry, because an IR library cannot move you across the room.

Sympathetic resonance

Gold bars are the energy sitting in each string — every strike adds to them and they leak away at that string's own measured decay rate. Bright gold is the ones loud enough to have been given a voice; dim gold is energy that is there but below the threshold or outside the voice limit. The lit background is every string whose damper is off, which is what the pedal actually does. Blue is what you are holding down. Press the pedal and play: the background lights up, then the bars build where the partials agree.

Resonance level per key

How strongly each string answers, in dB on top of the amount above: click to add a point, drag to move, double-click to remove, pull a point to the bottom to stop those strings answering at all. It ships flat, doing nothing. The point of it is the right-hand end: the top strings of a piano have no dampers (the green line is where they stop), so they are free to answer everything you play whether the pedal is down or not, and go on ringing after the rest of the instrument has been stopped. Taking them down here quietens them and shortens them — a string given less energy drops under the voice threshold sooner — which is the difference between this and turning the whole resonance down.
Which strings answer is decided by partial coincidence, with each string's real inharmonicity and the bandwidth implied by its measured decay — so a fifth answers strongly and a tritone barely. Selectivity widens that bandwidth: at 1× it is physical and almost nothing resonates unless it is in tune to a cent, which is why every instrument that does this has to open it up. Build with velocity is the exponent on velocity: 2 is the energy a hammer delivers. Soundboard feeds all of this into a long, dark reverb, so lifting the pedal does not cut the resonance dead: lifting it ducks the resonance down to pedal-off drop at once — the dampers touching — then lets it fall the rest of the way slowly over pedal-off fall, scattered slightly in time so the frame does not land as one click, while the body goes on ringing for the soundboard tail, the way a whole piano keeps sounding for a few seconds after the dampers land. Pedal-up bleed is how much a damped string still answers: a damper never fully stills a string and the soundboard couples them regardless, so a real grand has a faint pitched halo even with no pedal. Those strings ring only briefly, so it reads as body rather than as sustain; at zero only free strings resonate, as before.

Solo mutes everything else — the struck notes, their release and damper sounds — and leaves only the sympathetic voices and the soundboard, which is the one way to hear what this section is actually contributing while you set it. It is a monitoring switch, not a setting: it is never saved, and the instrument always comes up unsoloed.

Note attack

Zero by default and doing nothing — the recording already has a hammer in it. Raise it to take the knock off the front of a note.

Damper fall

Duration is per key — the Damper fall parameter above. This is the shape, and it is what decides whether a released chord sounds stopped or swallowed. The same shape stops a sympathetic voice, because it is the same damper.

Release sample attack

Release samples start on top of a note that is still sounding, so switching one on at full level is a click in the middle of a decaying chord.

Release level against how long the key was held

The damper-release sound is the sound of a damper stopping something, so it depends on how much is left to stop — and a string four seconds into its decay has very little. The dashed blue line is C4's own decay, measured at build time: trace it and the law is physically right for that key. Salamander's SFZ does this as a flat dB per second; a real decay is not a straight line, and the first second matters far more than the eighth. The key-up thud does not follow by default — a key comes up the same way however long it was down — but the last slider is there if you disagree.

Mechanics

The sustain pedal is continuous, not a switch: half-pedalling shortens a note without stopping it, and CC 64 is read across its whole range. The damper-fall sound is picked by velocity and scaled by how long the key was held — a string ringing for four seconds has less left in it to stop. The limiter is a seatbelt, not a sound: it is doing nothing until eighty-eight undamped strings would have run out of headroom. Align sample starts lines up the attack of all 1408 recordings: each one was measured (tools/sampler/align.mjs) for how far into the file its attack front actually is, and it runs from 5 ms to 59 ms across this library — the build trims each head at a level threshold, which lines up where silence ends, not where the note arrives. With this on, a late recording is started further in and an early one is held back by the difference, so every key's attack lands the same distance after the key goes down and the keyboard feels even. Nothing is re-cut: it is an offset at playback, and Sample start in the Parameters panel nudges one key or one range on top of it. Release start trim shaves a fixed few ms off the front of every release and damper sample; round-robin adds a random few ms on top of that per play, so the same key let go twice does not replay the identical file and machine-gun. Release delay holds the note a millisecond past the key-up so its damper falls in step with the release sample, not a hair ahead of it. There is no pedal-action sample: Salamander's two pedal recordings are a mechanism being worked, with a room and a frame ringing in them that are not this room or this frame, and what they stand in for — the whole undamped frame lighting up as the pedal goes down — is something this engine does properly, string by string, under Sympathetic resonance. They are not loaded.

Parameters — pick the keys once, then edit; the chart is a preview

Every slider moves whatever the strip at the top has selected — the whole instrument, a range you dragged, or one key — so there is one place where the question "which keys?" is answered. The three tiers add: a range edit is a departure from the global value, a key edit a departure from that, and zero always means "as shipped". A range is feathered at its edges, so raising the top octave and a half does not make one key suddenly different; set the edge fade to zero when you do want a hard edge, the way a real piano has one at the bass break. The charts on the right are previews, not editors — the gold line is the total across all three tiers, which is where a cliff shows up, and the caption names the biggest jump between neighbouring keys. Clicking one selects that key to edit. smooth rounds off the per-key layer only, so a deliberate single-key fix survives it unless you ask. Edits are stored in this browser and reload with the page.

Everything not in this list is one value for the instrument, because it is a property of the instrument rather than of a key: master, stereo spread and perspective, room, resonance, the envelope shapes, the EQ, and the mechanical noise levels. A Bézier shape cannot be a per-key offset — there is no meaningful "twenty percent of a curve" to add — but its duration can be, and the damper fall's already is: the shape is global, the time it takes is the Damper fall parameter here.
